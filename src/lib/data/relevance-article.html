Relevance begins with a connection between a question and an answer. Someone trying to understand why a form lost a lead needs a different explanation from someone choosing a developer to connect a website to a CRM. The subjects overlap, but the purpose of each visit is different.

A useful website makes those distinctions clear. It explains the subject on the page, gives the reader an answer suited to their task, and connects that answer to the technical detail or supporting example that belongs with it.

That is the idea behind TopoRank. I built it to examine the meaning of pages alongside the links between them. On October 31, 2026, TopoRank examined 183 pages on thewebguy.app and organized them into 40 topic groups. Inside those groups were some revealing relationships: service pages connected to the skills behind them, form problems connected to backend integrations, and closely related local pages serving different geographic needs.

The interesting part was how those relationships became visible. Page vocabulary, vector similarity, topic groups, and link context each explained a different part of the site's relevance.

Why meaning and intent matter

Search intent is what someone is trying to accomplish through a search. An explanation, a diagnosis, a comparison, and a request for help can all concern the same subject while requiring different content.

Google describes systems that interpret meaning and intent, match concepts in queries and pages, and analyze connections between documents. Its guidance on helpful content also emphasizes whether readers learn enough to achieve their goal. Together, those ideas explain why a page's relevance involves both its subject and the purpose it serves. Google's ranking-systems guide, Google's guidance on helpful content.

On a technical website, that purpose often changes as the reader learns more. “My form isn't working” may become “the form submits, but the CRM never receives the lead.” The second description is more specific. It connects the visible symptom to a different technical layer, and it changes which explanation is useful next.

That progression appears in the relationships TopoRank found.

Reading the words and the meaning

TopoRank's crawler collects HTML, extracts content and internal links, and records where those links appear. Navigation, footer, and main-content links retain different contexts in the analysis.

The content gets two complementary readings. The lexical analysis uses TF-IDF to identify informative terms within the collection. Words such as payload, endpoint, authentication, and retry make an integration page's subject explicit. The semantic analysis represents the text as an embedding vector, allowing pages with related meaning to be compared across different wording.

Clustering and additional heuristics organize the pages into topic groups, also called pillars. These groups describe the surrounding subject area. A service overview, a skill explanation, and a practical example can belong to one topic while playing different roles within it.

The link graph adds the connections between those documents. A link embedded in a discussion of a failed CRM handoff carries an explanation of why the destination belongs there. A navigation link supplies a more general route through the site. TopoRank records that difference and weighs repeated navigation and footer connections differently from links in the main content.

Google’s link guidance emphasizes descriptive anchor text and the words around the link. Together, they help explain what the destination contributes to the surrounding discussion. Google’s link best practices.

The math behind the page comparisons

A potential ranking opportunity can be flagged when the combined relevance score exceeds a chosen threshold:

O = { true if T > X false if T ≤ X

O = true if T > X; false otherwise.

O is the potential ranking opportunity flag, T is the combined score, and X is the chosen threshold on the same scale. Passing that threshold identifies a relevance-based opportunity; it does not predict a ranking position.

The combined score brings together the page’s wording, meaning and link support. Each contributes according to its weight:

T = w L ⁢ L + w V ⁢ V + w S ⁢ S

T = wLL + wVV + wSS

T is the combined score, L is lexical fit (wording), V is vector fit (meaning), and S is link support. The weights wL , wV and wS determine how much each contributes and add up to 1.

Lexical fit: comparing the words

Lexical fit examines vocabulary. For a page and its topic, TopoRank compares two lists of weighted words and phrases: the page’s vocabulary and the topic’s vocabulary. Terms that carry more information about the subject have more influence on the comparison.

L ( p , t ) = k p · k t ‖ k p ‖ ‖ k t ‖

L(p, t) = (kp · kt) / (‖kp‖ ‖kt‖)

kp represents the page’s weighted vocabulary, and kt represents the topic’s weighted vocabulary. The calculation compares their shared terms while accounting for the size of each list. A higher value means the page’s word usage is more closely aligned with that topic.

The Lexical number beside an individual search phrase answers a more direct question: how much of that phrase’s weighted vocabulary appears on the page?

L ( q , p ) = ∑ i ∈ Q ∩ P a i ∑ i ∈ Q a i

L(q, p) = Σi ∈ Q ∩ P ai / Σi ∈ Q ai

Q is the search phrase’s set of terms, P is the page’s vocabulary, and ai is the weight of a term. The top of the fraction counts the query terms found on the page; the bottom includes all query terms. The resulting 0–1 value is shown on a 0–100 scale in the query rows.

Vector fit: comparing the meaning

Vector fit examines whether the search phrase and page discuss the same subject, even when they use different words. Each is represented by a vector—a list of numbers describing its meaning. Cosine similarity compares the direction of those two vectors.

V ( q , p ) = max ( 0 , e q · e p ‖ e q ‖ ‖ e p ‖ )

V(q, p) = max(0, (eq · ep) / (‖eq‖ ‖ep‖))

eq represents the search phrase’s meaning, and ep represents the page’s meaning. A value closer to 1 means their directions are more alike. The query’s Vector number uses zero as its lower limit and displays the result on a 0–100 scale.

Page-to-page cosine uses the same direction comparison with two page vectors. Its full range is −1 to +1: opposite directions at −1, perpendicular directions at 0 and the same direction at +1. It is a different comparison from matching a search phrase to a page.

One worked example is the API integration service and REST API/webhook skill page: their measured cosine is 0.853678, displayed as (Topic grouping: Website Fixes And Troubleshooting) . The service describes a website-to-system handoff, while the skill explains payloads, endpoints, authentication and retries. Their shared work is expressed at different levels of detail.

Comparing relevance with Search Console data

The search phrases in these examples came from data pulled through Google’s Search Console API for thewebguy.app. The data connected each phrase with the page that appeared for it, alongside search impressions, clicks and average position. That gave the relevance comparisons a connection to searches in which the website had actually appeared.

The comparison covers four complete weeks, September 28–October 25, 2026, and includes 61 distinct page–query pairs across the first six pages. The three lower examples have no query rows returned for the same period. A phrase appearing for the same page in more than one week still counts as one pair. Each phrase was compared with its associated page’s wording, meaning and internal-link context.

Search Console and TopoRank explain different parts of that relationship. Search Console describes the page’s observed visibility for a phrase. TopoRank examines the relevance of the page to that phrase: whether the words are present, whether the meaning is related and how the page connects with the wider site. Reading them together helps explain why a search about checkout repair relates to a particular WooCommerce page, or why a location name changes the fit of an otherwise similar request.

The site-wide test at the end applies the same comparison period to all 183 scored pages. It checks whether each page has any disclosed query with an impression and compares that result with the page-level Topical Relevance Score. It is separate from the 61 phrase-level comparisons shown in the six worked examples.

The Topical Relevance Score is calculated by TopoRank; it is not a score supplied by Google. Comparing it with Search Console data places the measurement alongside observed search behavior, so the examples connect an explanation of relevance with evidence of visibility.

What relevance tells us about search visibility

Search visibility connects a page with the language people use to find information. The queries disclosed in Search Console show some of the searches in which that page appeared. Read alongside the page’s content, they reveal where the subject being searched and the explanation being offered meet—and where the connection is less certain.

The WooCommerce examples make that distinction visible. General store support and checkout repair share a platform and much of the same vocabulary. A search about a failed payment, however, describes a more specific problem than a search for WooCommerce support. The pages can be closely related in semantic space while answering different requests. Their similarity explains the shared subject; their content explains which request each page serves.

Location and the kind of help requested also affect the match. A phrase naming Freeport or Beloit gives a different geographic context from “near me,” while IT support can refer to more than website work. The words may overlap without establishing that the page answers every part of the request.

Internal links give those relationships a visible structure. A passage that connects a checkout failure to a checkout-repair page explains why that destination belongs in the discussion. A link back to broader store support explains how the narrower problem fits within the larger subject. This is the topological part of relevance: the meaning of an individual page sits within a network of explanations, with each connection supplying context about the others.

A search visibility opportunity is the overlap between a real search need and an explanation a page can meaningfully provide. The observed queries help describe that overlap; the content and link context explain its substance. These examples show why a page may be relevant to a family of searches without being equally relevant to every phrase in that family.

The three pages without observed queries add another distinction. Their content and links can relate to a subject even when no search phrase appears for them in the comparison. Topic fit describes the page; Search Console provides the observed page–query connection. Neither can stand in for the other.

The size of that opportunity is a separate question. The number of disclosed phrases does not describe the share of all searches, the amount of available traffic or the likelihood of ranking. TopoRank’s similarity and support scores describe the site’s content relationships. Neither measurement, on its own, establishes how much visibility the page will receive.

From page relevance to search opportunity

Relevance begins with the relationship between a question and an answer. The words on a page make its subject explicit, its meaning connects it with related searches, and its internal links show where that explanation belongs within the wider site. Together, they give a clearer account of what the page can answer and why a reader would arrive there.

The examples show why those relationships matter. WooCommerce support and checkout repair share a subject while serving different needs. The Freeport and Beloit pages connect a service with a particular place. A plugin-conflict page links a visible problem to the development work that can resolve it. In each case, the useful connection depends on more than shared vocabulary: it also depends on the task, the scope and the reason the next page belongs in the discussion.

The measurements help make those distinctions visible. Lexical fit describes the wording, vector fit describes the meaning, and link support describes the surrounding connections. Read alongside the actual queries and page content, they explain how a page can be closely related to a subject without answering every search about it equally well.

That is where relevance becomes a potential search opportunity: a search expresses a need that the page can meaningfully answer, and the surrounding pages reinforce its role. The numbers help identify that relationship; they do not guarantee a ranking. The value of topological relevance is understanding how the language, purpose and connections across a website come together to answer the searches it is suited to serve.
